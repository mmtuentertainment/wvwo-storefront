# Stonewall Jackson Lake: History and Cultural Context

## Executive Summary

Stonewall Jackson Lake is a 2,630-acre impoundment on the West Fork River in Lewis County, West Virginia, created as a multi-purpose flood control project by the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers. Authorized in 1966 and completed in 1988, the reservoir protects downstream communities including Weston, Clarksburg, Shinnston, and Fairmont from catastrophic flooding. However, the project's construction involved the displacement of approximately 1,800 residents, the submersion of communities including Brownsville and Roanoke, and remains controversial among local residents. The lake is named after Thomas Jonathan "Stonewall" Jackson (1824-1863), a prominent Confederate general whose naming has become increasingly contentious in recent years due to his role defending slavery.

---

## Part I: Dam Construction Timeline and Specifications

### Authorization and Planning

- **1966**: Project authorized by the federal Flood Control Act of 1966 for multiple purposes: flood control, water quality improvement, water supply, fish and wildlife habitat improvement, hydropower, and recreation
- **1980**: Planning phase begins
- **1980s**: Local opposition delays construction; residents vigorously opposed the project

### Construction Phase

- **1983-1986**: Construction begins (sources vary on exact start date; primary sources indicate mid-1980s commencement)
- **June 1988**: Dam completed and water storage begins
- **January 1989**: Gates closed; reservoir begins full operation as a permanent water storage facility
- **1990**: Stonewall Jackson Lake State Park completed and opened to public

### Dam Specifications

| Feature | Specification |
|---------|---------------|
| **Location** | West Fork River, Lewis County, West Virginia, near Weston |
| **Dam Length** | 620 feet |
| **Maximum Height** | 95 feet above stream bed |
| **Dam Type** | Earthen/concrete composite (107,000 cubic yards of concrete) |
| **Reservoir Size** | 2,630-2,650 acres at full pool |
| **Drainage Area** | 102 square miles |
| **Construction Cost** | $208 million (1980s dollars) |
| **Fill Material** | Earthen dam with concrete spillway components |

### Operational Status

- **Fully operational**: 1988 (25+ years of service as of 2013)
- **Primary function**: Flood control for Monongahela River system
- **Secondary functions**: Recreation, water supply, fish/wildlife habitat
- **Current jurisdiction**: U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, Pittsburgh District

## Part II: Purpose and Justification

### Multi-Purpose Design (Flood Control Act of 1966)

The Stonewall Jackson project was authorized for six concurrent purposes:

1. **Flood Control** (Primary)
   - Protects downstream communities from catastrophic inundation
   - Reduces peak flow rates on West Fork River
   - Mitigates damage from 100-year and 500-year flood events

2. **Water Quality Improvement**
   - Reduces downstream sedimentation
   - Provides water storage during drought periods
   - Moderates water temperature in West Fork River

3. **Water Supply**
   - Provides municipal and industrial water supply
   - Supports regional water demand during dry seasons
   - Supplements Monongahela River flow

4. **Fish and Wildlife Habitat**
   - Creates 2,630-acre lake ecosystem
   - Supports recreational fishing (bass, muskellunge, crappie)
   - Provides waterfowl and birdwatching habitat

5. **Hydropower**
   - Minor hydroelectric generation capability
   - Not primary focus but contributes to overall energy strategy

6. **Recreation**
   - Enables boating, fishing, swimming, camping
   - Creates economic development through state park system
   - Supports tourism in rural Lewis County

### Flood Protection Benefits (Documented Results)

- **As of 2013**: $423 million in prevented flood damage (25 years of operation)
- **Average annual benefit**: ~$17 million in flood damage prevention
- **Protected communities**: Weston, Clarksburg, Shinnston, and Fairmont, WV
- **Downstream reach**: Protects Monongahela River corridor to Pennsylvania

## Part III: Submerged Communities and Relocations

### Communities Inundated

#### Brownsville, West Virginia (Primary Submersion)

**Historical Context Before Flooding:**
- 1891: Village consisted of store, post office, and six homes
- Over 100 years: Approximately 20 families lived in Brownsville
- Late 1800s-early 1900s: Community expanded to include school and church
- 1964: School closed (two decades before inundation)
- 1972: Brownsville Community Church closed (16 years before water storage)
- 1988: Entire village submerged under lake water

**Commemoration:**
- The scenic overlook of Stonewall Jackson Lake dam was constructed using stone from the original Brownsville village bridge
- This transformation of village materials into dam infrastructure represents the physical merging of lost community identity with new water management structure

#### Roanoke, West Virginia (Submerged)

- Completely flooded in over 60 feet of water when dam gates closed in January 1989
- Despite Roanoke's erasure from the landscape, an exit off Interstate 79 still bears the town's name as historical marker
- Exit sign represents one of few remaining references to the vanished community

### Displacement Statistics

- **Total residents displaced**: Approximately 1,800 people
- **Land acquisition**: 20,000 acres purchased by federal government for project
- **Economic compensation**: Displacement occurred during 1980s; specific relocation assistance records not available in public sources
- **Relocation assistance**: Federal Uniform Relocation Assistance and Real Property Acquisition Policies Act (49 U.S.C. § 4601 et seq.) would have applied, though individual circumstances varied widely

### Notable Resistance Cases

#### Barbara Heavner (Documented Resistance)

- **Story**: One Lewis County resident, Barbara Heavner, refused to leave her home when informed the Stonewall Jackson dam would submerge her property
- **Property lost**: Farm and nursery business completely inundated by 1989
- **Historic significance**: Her story appears in audio documentary "We're Here To Take You Out" (created 1984-1985)
- **Broader context**: Heavner's case represents broader pattern of rural land dispossession and federal power over private property rights

### Audio Documentation of Displacement

**"We're Here To Take You Out" (1984-1985 oral history project)**
- **Format**: Audio interviews with 35 Lewis County residents, planners, politicians, and law enforcement
- **Documentation**: Recorded perspectives from people forced to relocate
- **Focus**: "Impact and collateral, human costs of such projects on rural life, arts, and values"
- **Preservation**: Stored as part of West Virginia historical archives
- **Significance**: Rare first-hand accounts of dam displacement trauma and rural community dissolution

### Cemetery Relocations

**Status**: Limited public documentation available

While the construction of Stonewall Jackson Lake displaced 1,800 people and required acquisition of 20,000 acres, specific records regarding cemetery relocations, grave movements, and burial site resituations are not extensively documented in readily available public sources. This represents a potential archival gap in the public historical record—a common pattern with large federal reservoir projects where burial relocations were completed but not comprehensively documented.

**Recommended research**: Contact U.S. Army Corps of Engineers Pittsburgh District archives or Lewis County historical records office for cemetery relocation documentation.

## Part IV: The Stonewall Jackson Namesake

### Thomas Jonathan "Stonewall" Jackson (1824-1863)

#### Biographical Overview

- **Birth date**: January 21, 1824, in Clarksburg, West Virginia
- **Childhood**: Raised at Jackson's Mill in Lewis County (uncle Cummins Jackson's property), 1831-1842
- **Education**: Appointment to U.S. Military Academy at West Point, 1842
- **Military career**: Union officer during Mexican-American War; Confederate general during American Civil War
- **Death date**: May 10, 1863 (killed at Battle of Chancellorsville)
- **Nickname**: "Stonewall" earned at First Battle of Manassas (July 1861)

#### Confederate Military Service

- **Theater**: Army of Northern Virginia under General Robert E. Lee
- **Notable battles**: First Manassas, Valley Campaign (1862), Antietam, Fredericksburg, Chancellorsville
- **Rank**: Lieutenant General (final rank)
- **Record**: Widely regarded as exceptional Confederate tactician; "Lee's right hand"

#### Connection to West Virginia Region

- **Childhood geography**: Lived in Lewis County and had family ties to Clarksburg area
- **Local reverence**: Became symbol of Appalachian Confederate identity in West Virginia
- **Cultural icon**: At least seven historical monuments/institutions named after him in West Virginia:
  - Stonewall Jackson Lake and Dam
  - Stonewall Jackson State Park/Resort
  - Stonewall Jackson statue at West Virginia State Capitol
  - Stonewall Jackson Middle School (renamed 2020)
  - Stonewall Jackson Memorial Hospital (Weston area)
  - Multiple historical markers

### The Naming Controversy (2020-Present)

#### Jackson's Slavery and Confederate Ideology

**Historical record on Jackson's views:**
- Jackson fought explicitly to preserve slavery and Confederate independence
- He owned slaves and depended on enslaved labor
- His ideology centered on "states' rights" doctrine, which was fundamentally about preserving slavery as a legal institution
- As a prominent general, he fought to perpetuate a system that enslaved Black people and denied them human rights

#### Recent Renaming Movements

**2020s trend: De-honoring Confederate symbolism**

- **Stonewall Jackson Middle School** (West Virginia): Renamed in 2020 as part of national reckoning with Confederate commemoration
- **Stonewall Jackson Cemetery** (Lexington, Virginia): Renamed "Oak Grove Cemetery" in September 2020
- **Stonewall Jackson Memorial Hospital** (Weston, West Virginia): Controversy over name; 2025 West Virginia Supreme Court case considered relocation/renaming

#### Arguments for Renaming Institutions

From West Virginia advocacy groups and historians:

> "Stonewall fought for a system that represented racism and inequality, viewing Black people as inherently lesser than others. A man who would lay down his life for such a cause has no business having a statue at our Capitol, let alone having a hospital, established to heal the sick and wounded, named after him." (West Virginia Watch, January 2024)

**Specific concerns:**
- Healing institutions (hospitals) should not bear names of those who fought to perpetuate oppression
- Public statuary should reflect current values of equality and human dignity
- Naming conventions perpetuate Lost Cause mythology in contemporary institutions

#### Counterarguments (Historical Preservation)

- Jackson represents Appalachian history and regional identity
- Renaming erases historical record and context
- Jackson's military achievements separate from slavery debate
- Name changes constitute "cancelation" of history

#### Current Status (as of 2026)

- **Stonewall Jackson Lake State Park/Resort**: Retains original name
- **Stonewall Jackson statue at State Capitol**: Subject of ongoing debate; not yet removed
- **Stonewall Jackson Memorial Hospital**: Complex naming case involved 2025 West Virginia Supreme Court litigation
- **Regional resistance**: Lewis County communities (self-identified as "Stonewall Country") maintain strong regional attachment to Jackson naming

### National Context

The Stonewall Jackson naming controversy reflects broader pattern of Confederate monument reconsideration across the United States (2015-present), particularly accelerated by the George Floyd uprising of 2020. West Virginia, unlike many southern states, has taken selective approach to renaming—some institutions changed names (schools), while others (major public landmarks) remain contested.

## Part V: West Fork River History and Significance

### Geographic and Hydrologic Overview

- **Classification**: Principal tributary of the Monongahela River
- **Length**: 103 miles (166 km) in north-central West Virginia
- **Formation**: Confluence of Straight Fork and Whites Camp Fork near Rock Cave, Upshur County
- **Drainage pattern**: Flows northwest through Lewis County and other counties toward Monongahela confluence
- **Elevation change**: Appalachian highlands to river valley

### Naming and Etymology

**Current name adoption**: "West Fork River" officially designated by U.S. Board on Geographic Names in 1902

**Historical names for same river:**
- Muddy River (Early colonial period)
- West Branch
- West Fork Monongahela River
- West River

**Etymology**: Purely descriptive—refers to western position as principal tributary of Monongahela River system (the Tygart constitutes the "East Fork")

**Native American etymology**: Name does not derive from indigenous language; no Native American place name origin documented (unlike many other West Virginia waterways)

### Commercial and Transportation History

#### Early 19th Century Development

- **1800**: West Fork River declared a navigable public highway by Virginia (predecessor state)
- **1793**: Virginia chartered company to maintain river navigation
- **Navigation requirements**: State law required dam operators (millers, manufacturers) to provide chutes allowing boat passage downstream
- **Economic purpose**: River navigation supported commerce, timber transport, and agricultural goods movement

#### Jackson's Mill (Historic Site)

- **Establishment**: Approximately 1800 by Edward Jackson (grandfather of Stonewall Jackson)
- **Structure**: Included residence, grist mill, and sawmill
- **Innovation**: Eastern mill and sawmill built in 1809 on riverside
- **Significance**: Stonewall Jackson lived there 1831-1842 during formative childhood years
- **Current status**: Converted to 4-H camp (1921, first in nation) and now WVU Jackson's Mill Center for Lifelong Learning

### Ecological and Recreational Significance

- **Fish species**: Supports populations of bass, muskellunge, crappie, and other freshwater species
- **Watershed**: Supports multiple communities downstream
- **Dam impact**: Stonewall Jackson Lake creates impoundment environment that alters native river ecology (benefits recreational fishing, impacts migratory species)
- **Paddling**: West Fork River offers kayaking and canoeing opportunities in unimpounded sections

## Part VI: Civil War History in the Region

### Lewis County During American Civil War

#### Divided Loyalties (1861-1865)

**Population division:**
- **Union sympathizers**: Greater majority (less affluent population), loyal to Federal government
- **Confederate sympathizers**: Small elite including doctors, lawyers, merchants with roots in eastern Virginia
- **Motivations**: Stonewall Jackson's prominence as Confederate general gave hope to local Confederates; his West Virginia origins provided symbolic connection

#### Military Occupation Pattern

- **Control**: Alternately occupied by Union and Confederate forces
- **Strategic position**: Lewis County sat in contested border region between western Virginia Unionist areas and Confederate-held territories
- **Infrastructure damage**: Repeated occupation/reoccupation caused civilian disruption

#### Specific Civil War Incidents

**Gold Seizure (June 1861):**
- Construction of Weston Hospital commenced 1859
- June 1861: Federal troops seized Virginia-deposited gold from Exchange Bank of Weston (~$30,000 in gold)
- Purpose of seizure: Funds transferred to Wheeling to support Reorganized Government of Virginia (pro-Union government formed after Virginia secession)
- **Historical irony**: Gold stolen from Weston bank became foundation fund for creating West Virginia state
- **Documentation**: Federal soldiers from Seventh Ohio Infantry conducted seizure

#### Confederate Military Organization in Lewis County

Local Confederate companies formed during war:
- Company I, 3rd Regiment Virginia State Line
- Company I, 18th Regiment Virginia Cavalry
- Company B, 26th Regiment Virginia Cavalry
- Company I (Lewis County Rangers), 31st Regiment Virginia Infantry

**Academic reference**: Roy Bird Cook, *Lewis County in the Civil War, 1861-1865* (Jarrett Printing Company, 1924) provides comprehensive analysis of Lewis County's Civil War experience

#### Weston as Strategic Location

- **Position**: County seat of Lewis County on West Fork River
- **Military value**: Control of Weston provided access to river valley transportation and communication routes
- **Hospital implications**: Construction of mental health facility (later Trans-Allegheny Lunatic Asylum) was delayed by Civil War and did not commence until post-war period

## Part VII: Native American History

### Pre-Contact Period (10,000 BCE - 1500 CE)

West Virginia, including Lewis County region:

**Archaeological evidence:**
- **Earliest habitation**: 10,000-14,000 years before present (Paleo-Indian period)
- **Megafauna hunting**: Early peoples hunted woolly mammoths and sabertooth cats
- **Mound building**: Evidence of complex societies 2,500 years ago constructing ceremonial mounds
- **Continuous occupation**: Archaeological record demonstrates human presence throughout pre-contact period

### Historic Contact Period (1600-1800)

**Historic tribal territories in region:**
- **Shawnee**: Primary historical presence in Ohio River valley, occasionally in West Virginia hunting territories
- **Lenni Lenape** (Delaware): Mid-Atlantic confederation presence
- **Cherokee**: Southern Appalachian and trans-Appalachian hunting grounds
- **Seneca**: Northern confederation extending into West Virginia
- **Tuscarora**: Iroquoian confederation members
- **Mingo**: Multilingual confederation in Ohio valley and Appalachian regions
- **Susquehannock**: Chesapeake drainage confederation

### Population Disappearance (Late 1600s-1700s)

**The "Indian Vacancy" Period:**

Before European American settlement expansion, Native populations essentially vanished from what is now West Virginia.

**Documented causes:**
- **Tribal conflict**: Intertribal warfare and competition for hunting territories and trade goods
- **European disease**: Smallpox, measles, and other pathogens traveled ahead of European settlement and devastated Native populations
- **Population displacement**: Surviving populations migrated to Great Lakes, Ohio River valley, or southern territories
- **Economic disruption**: European goods and trade systems disrupted traditional economies

**Timing**: By 1750-1770, most of West Virginia was depopulated of permanent Native settlements, though used as hunting grounds by external tribes

### Scholarly Corrections to Historical Narratives

**Previous misconception**: West Virginia used only as "Indian hunting ground"

**Current scholarship**:
- West Virginia supported permanent Native American settlements for 10,000+ years
- Complex societies with mound-building, agriculture, and substantial populations
- Not "empty wilderness" awaiting European settlement
- Population displacement caused by colonialism, disease, and warfare—not natural depopulation

### Lewis County Specific History

- **Tribal use**: Part of broader Appalachian region used for hunting by multiple tribal nations
- **No indigenous place names**: West Fork River and surrounding region do not have documented Native American etymology
- **Post-depopulation**: By 1790s, European settlement began with no surviving Native populations

### Contemporary Native American Population

**Current status:**
- **Federally recognized tribes**: Zero federally recognized tribes with primary territory in West Virginia
- **State recognition**: No state-recognized tribes in West Virginia
- **Population claiming ancestry**: Approximately 11,000 West Virginia residents claim Native American ancestry (2010 Census)
- **Tribal affiliations**: Descendants and heritage members may claim connections to Shawnee, Cherokee, Seneca, Delaware, or Mingo heritage

## Part VIII: Local Culture and Contemporary Activities

### Weston, West Virginia (County Seat)

#### Historical Development

- **Founding**: 1817 as Preston (original name)
- **Renaming**: Fleshersville (intermediate), then Weston (1819, permanent)
- **County seat**: Lewis County governmental center
- **Population**: County seat of ~15,000 person county (as of 2020 Census)

#### Twentieth Century Development

**Industrial heritage:**
- Natural gas discoveries at turn of 20th century (1890s-1900s)
- Weston became glass-manufacturing center, particularly blown glass
- **Historic claim**: Lewis County was "hand blown glass capital of the world" during industrial heyday
- **Modern status**: Appalachian Glass offers daily demonstrations of dying craft art

**Mental health infrastructure:**
- 1858: Weston selected as Virginia's third mental health hospital site
- Construction delayed by Civil War (1861-1865)
- **Trans-Allegheny Lunatic Asylum**: Largest hand-cut stone masonry building in North America
- **Architectural significance**: Gothic revival style, constructed with local stone
- **Modern status**: Now historic site offering tours; significant tourism draw

#### Contemporary Weston

- **Antique markets**: Multiple antique shops and markets
- **Historic home tours**: Seasonal tours of Victorian-era architecture
- **Tourism brand**: "Lewis County CVB" (Convention and Visitors Bureau) markets region as "Stonewall Country"
- **Visitor attractions**: Mix of Civil War heritage, architectural history, and Appalachian culture

### Stonewall Jackson Lake State Park/Resort

#### Recreational Infrastructure

**Water-based activities:**
- Boating (sailboats, motorboats, pontoons)
- Fishing (recreational fishing for bass, muskellunge, crappie)
- Kayaking and canoeing
- Stand-up paddle boarding
- Paddleboard rentals and aqua cycles
- Lake cruises (The Little Sorrel, 70x16 ft cruiser named after Stonewall Jackson's horse)

**Land-based activities:**
- Hiking (13 miles of maintained trails around lake)
- Golf (Arnold Palmer Signature Course)
- Escape rooms (puzzle-solving indoor attractions)
- Camping (tent and RV facilities)
- Picnicking and day-use areas

#### Annual Events and Festivals

**Holiday/seasonal celebrations:**
- Memorial Day Weekend: Family activities and live music
- July 4th: Fireworks over lake (major draw)
- Labor Day Weekend: Family activities (Jackson's Mill Jubilee held at Jackson's Mill)
- Hunting and Fishing Show (annual)
- Specialty brew and cider tastings

**Jackson's Mill Jubilee** (separate from resort, Labor Day):
- Heritage crafts demonstrations
- Historical reenactments
- Music and cultural performances
- Celebrates Appalachian tradition and regional history

#### Hospitality and Conference Services

- Wedding venues
- Conference hosting
- Multi-room accommodations at Stonewall Resort
- Full-service dining
- Destination resort status for regional and regional-to-state visitors

### Regional Tourism Branding

**"Stonewall Country" Initiative (Lewis County CVB):**

Lewis County Convention & Visitors Bureau markets region as "Stonewall Country":
- **Primary attractions**: Historic sites (Jackson's Mill, Trans-Allegheny Asylum, Weston historic district), outdoor recreation (lake, hiking, fishing)
- **Target market**: Families, retirees, outdoor enthusiasts, history tourism
- **Seasonal promotion**: Four-season destination marketing
- **Economic strategy**: Position rural Lewis County as outdoor recreation destination within 3-hour drive of major Appalachian metro areas

### Appalachian Cultural Identity

#### Regional Self-Identification

- **Motto**: "Stonewall Country"—regional identity centered on Jackson heritage, rugged individualism, outdoor recreation
- **Values**: Self-sufficiency, hunting/fishing traditions, family, craftsmanship
- **Appalachian resilience narrative**: Communities that adapted after dam displacement and industrial decline

#### Arts and Crafts Heritage

- **Appalachian Glass**: Hand-blown glass tradition maintained at demonstration center in Weston
- **4-H Center at Jackson's Mill**: Youth development and heritage skills training
- **Local artisans**: Craft communities throughout Lewis County

#### Seasonal and Cultural Events

- Jackson's Mill Jubilee (Labor Day)
- Hunting seasons (deer, turkey, upland game)
- Fishing seasons
- Winter holiday celebrations at state park

## Part IX: Controversy and Modern Debates

### Dam Removal Advocacy

Despite flood control benefits, environmental groups have questioned continued operation:

**Arguments for dam removal:**
- **Aquatic ecosystem impact**: Dam blocks fish migration and alters river habitat
- **Water quality**: Impounded water creates conditions unsuitable for certain native species
- **Restoration**: Removing dam could restore West Fork River to pre-impoundment conditions
- **Cost-benefit**: 35+ years post-construction allows reassessment of cost/benefit

**Current status**: No active dam removal plans as of 2026; dam continues standard operation

### Displacement Legacy

**Ongoing community impact:**
- Multigenerational trauma from forced relocations (1986-1989)
- Lost communities (Brownsville, Roanoke) exist only in memory and Interstate 79 signage
- Audio documentation ("We're Here To Take You Out") preserves oral histories but project remains controversial in local memory

### Federal Power and Private Property Rights

The Stonewall Jackson project exemplifies broader pattern of federal eminent domain use for resource development:
- Government authority to acquire private land for "public benefit"
- Limited compensation for displaced residents and lost livelihoods
- Resistance by landowners (Barbara Heavner case) ultimately unsuccessful against federal power

### Monument/Naming Reckoning

The Stonewall Jackson naming—of lake, resort, state park, and multiple institutions—sits at intersection of:
- **Regional pride**: Jackson's connection to Lewis County generates local support for naming retention
- **National reckoning**: 2020s broader movement to remove Confederate commemoration
- **Identity politics**: "Stonewall Country" branding ties regional economics to Jackson identity

## Part X: Economic and Social Impact Assessment

### Positive Economic Impacts (Post-1990)

- **Recreation economy**: State park generates lodging, dining, retail revenue
- **Real estate**: Lake properties command premium prices; waterfront development
- **Employment**: Park operations, resort staff, marina operators, guides
- **Tourism**: $40+ million annual tourism impact to Lewis County (estimated)
- **Flood prevention**: Documented $423 million in prevented flood damage (25 years)

### Negative Social Impacts (Displacement Era)

- **Community dissolution**: Loss of Brownsville, Roanoke, and other smaller settlements
- **Agricultural displacement**: 20,000 acres removed from farming/timber use
- **Economic disruption**: 1,800 residents forced relocation during economically vulnerable 1980s
- **Psychological trauma**: Documented in oral histories ("We're Here To Take You Out")
- **Intergenerational trauma**: Families still living in Lewis County maintain memory of displacement

### Contemporary Context

Lewis County now exists in paradox:
- **Economic dependence**: State park generates vital tourism revenue for economically distressed rural county
- **Historical resentment**: Communities that lost land and family homes now depend on economic activity generated by that same reservoir
- **Identity consolidation**: "Stonewall Country" branding transformed trauma into regional brand, but at cost of erasing displaced communities from public consciousness

## Summary of Key Findings

### Timeline
The Stonewall Jackson Lake project followed a 22-year arc from initial planning to full operational status: authorized in 1966, planned through the 1970s-early 1980s, constructed 1983-1988, and fully operational by 1989. The project represents one of the largest federal water management interventions in West Virginia history.

### Submerged History
The creation of a 2,630-acre reservoir required the displacement of approximately 1,800 residents and acquisition of 20,000 acres. Communities including Brownsville and Roanoke were completely submerged, with the only remaining physical artifact being the Brownsville bridge stones repurposed for the scenic overlook. The displacement remains documented in oral histories collected during the 1984-1985 period, preserving the human dimensions of federal engineering projects often erased from technical documentation.

### Cultural Complexity
While the lake has generated significant recreational and economic benefits to the region, the naming after Confederate General Stonewall Jackson has become increasingly controversial in the context of 2020s national reckoning with Confederate commemoration. Jackson's explicit support for slavery and the Confederacy conflicts with contemporary values, yet his regional significance and the "Stonewall Country" branding make renaming politically difficult.

### Ongoing Significance
Stonewall Jackson Lake represents a pivotal intersection of federal power, environmental management, regional identity, historical trauma, and contemporary values. For WVWO's Stonewall Lake destination content, this context should inform sensitive, historically honest presentation that acknowledges both the recreational opportunities and the community displacement that made the lake possible.
